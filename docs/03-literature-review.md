# 3. Literature Review

## 3.1 Scope and method

The review covers the four parts of the topic — **microlearning, short (AI-generated) video, adaptive knowledge assessment and social learning** — and the evidence on AI tutoring and feedback that the design depends on. Planning, gamification and privacy are reviewed as supporting themes.

- **Sources:** 28, of which 27 are peer-reviewed scientific publications and 20 are from 2021–2026. Eight earlier works (1995–2020) are kept because current models and principles build on them. Details are in §3.6.
- **Verification:**
  - every DOI was resolved through the Crossref API on 21 October 2026 and checked against title, authors, venue and pages;
  - each DOI was also checked for retractions and corrections;
  - content was read from the full text (17 sources) or the abstract (11), marked [FT] / [Abs];
  - all numbers are taken from the sources.
- **Search:** Crossref, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, Europe PMC and arXiv. The log, criteria and reserve list are in §3.5.

Theme letters: **A** microlearning · **B** short video and multimedia design · **C** AI-generated video and content · **D** adaptive assessment and knowledge tracing · **E** retrieval practice and spacing · **F** social and collaborative learning · **G** generative-AI tutoring · **H** feedback and LLM grading · **I** procrastination and time management · **J** gamification · **K** privacy and governance.

## 3.3 Critical synthesis by theme

**A. Microlearning (L1, L2).** Recent reviews agree that microlearning means short, single-objective units delivered in seconds to minutes, increasingly on mobile devices, and that it is associated with gains in knowledge, retention, engagement and motivation. However, both reviews are qualitative syntheses. Neither computes a pooled effect size, and the evidence is dominated by medical and workplace training and by studies from 2015–2024. Searches surfaced several "microlearning meta-analyses" (2025–2026), but only in low-visibility outlets, so they were excluded. The lack of a rigorous meta-analysis is itself a finding that YeahGrind's pilot can address. The design takeaways are concrete: one objective per unit, duration matched to the student's declared available minutes, interactive rather than passive content, and screens designed for phones. YeahGrind therefore measures its own effect with pre/post tests instead of assuming a pooled effect size (09 §9.5).

**B. Short educational video and multimedia design (L3, L4, L5).** Three strands of evidence converge. Large-scale logs show that attention to video drops after roughly six minutes (L3). Randomised trials in higher education show that video helps most when it is added to existing teaching (g = 0.80) rather than substituted for it (g = 0.28) (L4). Four decades of experiments yield 15 multimedia principles for reducing extraneous load, managing essential load and fostering generative processing (L5). The main tension is that the engagement evidence (L3) uses watch-time proxies from 2012 MOOCs, while the learning evidence (L4, L5) comes from controlled studies with their own publication and laboratory biases. Neither strand tests the 30–120-second vertical clips typical of modern short-form video apps. For YeahGrind this means generated clips must be short, segmented, coherent and conversational, and always paired with practice. The product should report learning per clip, not only views.

**C. AI-generated video and LLM-generated content (L6, L7, L8).** Early controlled studies suggest that AI-generated teaching videos and synthetic presenters produce learning comparable to human-made videos: no difference in gains with n = 83 (L7), and similar exam results with 447 participants (L6). Learners, however, rate the human-made experience slightly higher (L6). LLM-generated quiz items reach near-human quality: 81.7% passed every criterion, and alignment with learning objectives was better than for human items. The remaining failures are characteristic: more items with multiple correct options and more give-away distractors (L8). All three studies are short-term, single-domain and use immediate tests. None examines delayed retention, students' trust when the AI origin is disclosed, or the errors that end-to-end automated generation can introduce. YeahGrind should therefore treat generated content as a draft that passes automatic checks and learner reporting, label it as AI-generated, and evaluate it with delayed quizzes.

**D. Adaptive assessment, knowledge tracing and personalized sequencing (L9–L13).** There are three families of approach. Bayesian knowledge tracing (L9) estimates per-skill mastery with interpretable parameters. Elo-style rating (L10) estimates student skill and item difficulty together with minimal machinery. Deep knowledge tracing and ML-based computerised adaptive testing (L11, L12) offer accuracy and efficiency but need large calibrated data. The surveys show that published accuracy comparisons are hard to interpret because dataset versions and terminology are inconsistent (L11). The recommender-systems review shows that most educational recommenders are evaluated on accuracy and satisfaction, and only 37.5% on pedagogical effectiveness (L13). For a new platform with few users, the evidence favors interpretable, data-light models: Elo for ability, item difficulty and placement, a mastery threshold for progression, and transparent rule-based scoring for the trajectory. Two limitations must be acknowledged. Elo needs about 100 students per item for stable difficulty and gives no statistical guarantees (L10), so all assessment must stay low-stakes. And the evaluation must include learning gains, not only click-through.

**E. Retrieval practice and spaced repetition (L14, L15).** The classroom testing effect is robust: 222 studies with 48,478 students give g = 0.499 (L14), and it is strongest with corrective feedback, repeated tests and a format consistent with the final assessment. Machine-learned spacing (half-life regression) outperformed heuristic schedulers in predicting recall (45%+ lower error) and raised engagement by 12% at scale (L15). The evidence is less settled on how these effects transfer from vocabulary and classroom quizzes to open-ended, LLM-graded open answers. HLR was also trained on millions of interactions that YeahGrind will not have at launch. The practical consequence is to make retrieval with feedback a mandatory step after every video or micro-unit, to start with a simple Leitner-type schedule, and to log the variables needed to train a model-based scheduler later.

**F. Social, collaborative and peer learning online (L16, L17).** Structured collaboration works. Computer-supported collaboration scripts improve domain learning (g = 0.24) and especially collaboration skills (g = 0.72) without the feared loss of motivation (L17). In online courses, social presence correlates only weakly with actual learning (r = .199), but more strongly with perceived learning (r = .432) and satisfaction (r = .447). Teaching presence has the strongest link with achievement (r = .353) (L16). Mixed populations and correlational designs limit the conclusions, and neither source studies informal peer communities of the kind YeahGrind proposes. The design therefore gives study groups scripted roles and shared unit goals rather than an open chat. The thesis should frame social features as supporting engagement and satisfaction unless the pilot measures achievement directly.

**G. Generative-AI tutoring (L18, L19, L20).** The strongest RCT evidence shows that a carefully engineered AI tutor can beat in-class active learning (effect 0.63–1.3 SD, N = 194). That tutor embedded expert step-by-step solutions in its prompts and enforced sequential scaffolding (L18). A field experiment with about 1,000 students shows the reverse risk: unrestricted GPT-4 access improved assisted practice but **lowered later unassisted exam scores by 17%**, while a hint-only tutor removed this harm (L19). Methodological critique warns that much of the fast-growing ChatGPT literature cannot attribute gains to the AI, because treatments, control conditions and learning measures are poorly specified (L20). A 2025 meta-analysis reporting g = 0.867 was retracted in April 2026 and was excluded for that reason. For YeahGrind, the defensible position is "guardrailed, grounded, scaffolded AI": hints instead of answers, hidden reference solutions, and unassisted mastery checks. The project's own evaluation should use an active control and a delayed test.

**H. Formative feedback and LLM grading (L21, L22).** Feedback is among the strongest influences on learning (average 0.79 across 12 meta-analyses). It is also volatile: about a third of effects in one large meta-analysis were negative, and praise about the self is the least effective form (L21). This explains why YeahGrind's feedback text must answer feed-up, feed-back and feed-forward at the task, process and self-regulation levels. LLM graders reach moderate agreement with humans, are reliable at the extremes (GPT-4 precision 0.91 for fully correct answers), and are inconsistent on partially correct answers, especially when the rubric key is short. They can also be biased if prior grades leak into the prompt (L22). A key limitation is that L22 compared against a single human grader in medicine. YeahGrind's grading of open answers and code (code is never executed) is therefore positioned as **formative and appealable**, with detailed hidden rubrics and extra review near the 80/100 threshold.

**I. Procrastination and time management (L23, L24).** Procrastination is best predicted by task aversiveness, delay, low self-efficacy and impulsiveness (L23). Time-management behaviors correlate moderately with achievement (r = .262) and more strongly with lower procrastination (r = .490) and better wellbeing (r = .313) (L24). Both bodies of evidence are correlational. L24 notes that most time-management studies are cross-sectional and that interventions are poorly described, so planning features cannot be assumed to cause better grades. Recent intervention-level evidence for digital planners in higher education remains thin. YeahGrind therefore uses these sources to motivate design choices (small actions, immediate feedback, energy-matched difficulty, deadline visibility), not to predict GPA effects. Wellbeing and procrastination self-reports belong in the pilot measures.

**J. Gamification (L25, L26).** Meta-analytic evidence supports moderate cognitive benefits of gamification (g = .49), which hold in rigorous studies. Motivational and behavioral effects are smaller and less stable, and game fiction and social interaction act as moderators (L25). Streak mechanics carry a documented side effect: highlighting a broken streak reduces later engagement, especially when users blame themselves, while allowing repairs softens the effect (L26). That evidence comes from consumer settings, so its size in student learning apps is unknown. The design implication is to keep XP, levels and avatar narrative tied to collaborative goals, avoid loss-framed penalties, and treat any streak display as optional and repairable.

**K. Privacy, ethics and governance (L27, L28).** Students' willingness to share learning data depends on their perceived risk and perceived control: higher control increases trust, and higher risk increases withholding (L27). UNESCO's guidance adds governance requirements for generative AI: data protection, a minimum age of 13 for independent use, protection of learner agency against over-reliance, validation of outputs, and no delegation of high-stakes decisions to AI (L28). The empirical base here is a single small Swedish sample (L27), and the guidance is policy rather than evidence (L28), so the requirements additionally follow the Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 94-V on personal data (06, NFR-06). For YeahGrind these sources justify explicit opt-in analytics, data export and deletion, AI-content labeling, formative-only AI grades with human appeal, and minimizing personal data sent to LLM providers.
